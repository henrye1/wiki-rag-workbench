# Wiki schema
Schema version 3.17
This document tells the wiki maintainer (the LLM) how this wiki is structured. Edit it to fit the domain.

## Purpose
A persistent, interlinked knowledge base compiled from the published standard text, organised to clause level. Pages are written and maintained by the engine; humans curate sources and ask questions. No page body is hand-written.
Each claim is a concise statement of what its source says, linked by its clause citation to the passage it rests on, so it can always be traced back to the original wording. A claims store holding each claim with its quote, character offsets, statement kind, force and claim ID is not built yet (a future feature); until it is, a claim is a cited sentence on a page, and the rules below that name quotes, offsets or claim IDs apply when the store is built. A claim may be shorter than its source but never changes its meaning: it keeps who the statement applies to and whether it is required, permitted, expected or only illustrated. Where the two differ, the source wording governs.
Every claim is labelled by kind — requirement, application guidance, implementation guidance, example, or editorial — and a claim is stated as an obligation only where its source wording is one ("shall", "must", "is required to"). Claims that cannot be supported by their source are kept off the pages.
Every sentence on every page that states something from a source ends with the clause it comes from, written [[source-<source-name>|<clause reference>]], on every page without exception. Sentences about the wiki itself are the only exception, as set out in Writing page text.
Four further rules apply on every page without exception. A sentence from application guidance, implementation guidance, an example, an editorial note, the introduction or the basis for conclusions opens with what it is: "Application guidance states ...", "The implementation guidance states ...", "In Example <n>, ...", "The editorial note states ...", "The introduction states ...", "The dissenting opinion of <member> states ..." or "The <issuer> concluded ...", and one from an agenda decision printed as an editorial note "The <issuing body> concluded ...", "The <issuing body> noted ...", "The <issuing body> observed ...", "The <issuing body> decided ...", "The <issuing body> emphasised ..." or "The editorial note states ...". A sentence from the Standard's own paragraphs has no opener, whatever its force. Every source paragraph is written up in full: its numbers, thresholds, time periods, conditions and exceptions are stated, and a paragraph with lettered or numbered parts keeps every part with the source's own lettering ((a), (b), (i), (ii)). All fifteen section pages are created on every ingest, including those that end up empty. A link points only to a page that exists; other standards and documents are written as plain text.

## Page types
There are eight page types, and the vocabulary is fixed rather than minted per document, so the same structure exists for every standard and nothing drifts between ingests. Every page is created from a controlled list or a saved question, never from free text or from whatever a source happened to say.
Only topic and subtopic pages hold claims. Every other page type is composed: it holds no claims of its own and is built from links to claims already filed, so nothing is stated twice in different words. Any page that is not a requirement says so.
- overview — the single top-level page, slug overview. Lists the fifteen sections with what each covers and whether it holds requirements or non-authoritative material. The overview and the page list (each page's title and one-line summary) are where the librarian starts when choosing where to look. No second top-level page is created, under the framework slug (e.g. ifrs-9) or any other name.
- source — one per registered source document, slug source-<name>. States what the document is (issuer, edition, part), its authority sentence transcribed verbatim, and which sections it informs. Dates and counts are stated only when the engine supplies them; they are never estimated. An issuer, edition or part that is not known is left out, never guessed. Key takeaways are at most five of the source's own highest-ranked claims, linked, not a written summary. The source page holds only these parts; it has no other sections.
- entity — another standard, interpretation or document that the pages name. Created only from the Checks list Document names, never from free text, and built by the engine from the pages, never written by the model. Every entity page lists, one line each, every claim on a topic or subtopic page that names it, as its clause citation, the source heading the clause sits under and a link to the page that holds it, grouped by section, as Checks (Entity lines) sets out; the section shows the nature of the link (scope, disclosure, measurement). It holds no claim text. Parties in examples (Bank A, Entity B) are not entities; they stay inside their example claims.
- concept — a term or subject the standard uses: a defined term, or a major heading of the standard's own text. Created only from the lists in Checks (Concept pages), never from free text, and built by the engine from the pages, never written by the model. A term's page states that the standard defines it and links to its definition in Definitions rather than copying it. Every concept page lists, one line each, every claim on a topic or subtopic page about the concept, as its clause citation, the source heading the clause sits under and a link to the page that holds it, grouped by section, as Checks (Concept lines) sets out. It holds no claim text.
- topic — one of the fifteen fixed sections. Created from the vocabulary before any claim is written, so an empty section is a page that says it is empty, not a missing page. A claim that names a section outside the fifteen is discarded and counted, never defaulted onto whichever page happens to be first.
- subtopic — part of one section, used where a section is split, as Checks sets out. It holds claims and follows every rule a topic page follows. The section's brief sets how it is split, and each subtopic page is named after a group taken from the fixed vocabulary or from the source's own headings, never invented: its slug is the section slug followed by the slugified group name (<section-slug>-<group>). A subtopic page is created only when at least one paragraph is routed to it, or when the size split (Checks: Page size limit) moves write-ups to it. A page that holds several groups is named after every heading it holds, in document order; a page split again is named only after the headings it holds, never after the page it was split from. It is not a navigation target on the overview; it is reached from its section page, which becomes an index: one line per item, saying what it covers and which clauses it cites, grouped by subtopic page, with no full write-ups.
- comparison — not built yet (a future feature): a side-by-side view of two or more things from the standard (e.g. two measurement bases; a requirement and the example that illustrates it). Each row links to the claims it rests on.
- synthesis — an analysis or answer filed back from a query. Records the question and date, and is never used as a source for new claims.

## The fifteen sections
Each brief states what belongs on the page, what to hand off and where, the boundary against the section most likely to be confused with it, and what makes a weak source for it. Assessing a source against its "Weak source if" line, and recording the result, is not built yet (a future feature); the lines are guidance to the writer until then. The whole of each brief is sent to the extraction model, which is what routes a claim to a page.
Every claim filed to any section carries its statement kind — requirement, application guidance, implementation guidance, example or editorial — read from the clause its quote sits in. Editorial annotations, and material the issuer publishes alongside the standard but outside it (such as agenda decisions or interpretive notes), are never filed as requirements, whichever section they bear on.
Any section can be split by size as Checks sets out; a split section's page is the index of its subtopic pages.
### 01. Overview and status
- Belongs here: what the standard is and where it stands — objective as the issuer states it, issue history (from the introduction the issuer prints before the Standard, cited by its page label and opened "The introduction states ..."), each amendment with its date and subject, effective date of the version documented, what it superseded and what has superseded it, the structural map of its published parts, and the authority sentence transcribed verbatim ("X is set out in paragraphs ... and ...") (or, where an appendix states its own authority, that statement, e.g. "This appendix is an integral part of the Standard").
- The authority sentence decides which parts bind. Worked examples and illustrations read their authority from it; record it exactly, because a misread here misfiles every example.
- For an interpretation: the question it resolves and the standard it interprets, stated before anything else. That question is the point of the document.
- Hand off: transition mechanics -> Transition and effective date (this section holds the dates, that one holds the procedure). What the standard sits alongside -> Interactions and application in practice.
- Not the overview page: this is a topic section holding claims; the overview page is the index of the fifteen and holds none.
- Weak source if: the authority sentence is paraphrased rather than transcribed, or the amendment chronology stops short of the edition being documented.
### 02. Scope and applicability
- Belongs here: whether the standard applies to something. Inclusions and exclusions, each exclusion named with the standard that picks it up instead, entities or transactions exempted, recognition exemptions taken by election, elections that move an item into or out of scope, and items sitting outside the main requirements but inside one sub-model — named here with the reason, treated elsewhere.
- The population of each sub-model (for example, the items to which one set of requirements applies) is written up in the section that owns it, not copied here. This section names each sub-model and links to the section that holds its population, so that scope questions can be answered from one place.
- Exclusions and their exceptions keep their conditions word for word ("except", "unless", "other than"). An exception to an exclusion is recorded with the exclusion it qualifies.
- Hand off: how this standard relates to another once both apply -> Interactions and application in practice. What the thing being accounted for actually is -> Unit of account. Treatment of anything in scope -> the functional section that owns it.
- Boundary: scope decides WHETHER the standard applies; unit of account decides WHAT it applies to.
- Weak source if: an exclusion is recorded without naming where the excluded item goes, or a partial-scope item is described without a pointer to the section holding its treatment, or a sub-model's population is listed incompletely.
### 03. Definitions
- Belongs here: every term the standard defines, verbatim with its reference; terms defined in the body rather than the glossary, flagged as such; and — equally important — terms of art the standard deliberately leaves undefined, recorded explicitly as open. Where another standard defines the same word differently, note it and link. Where this standard borrows a glossary wholesale, record the borrowing rather than leaving the section blank.
- Each defined or open term creates or updates its concept page, which links back to the definition here.
- Wording that appears only in an editorial annotation is not a definition and is not recorded here.
- Hand off: the entity's decision on an open term -> Judgements, estimates and policy choices. Terms the firm or a client defines -> methodology/research or client working papers, never here.
- Weak source if: a term of art is used without recording whether the standard defines it, presumes it, or leaves it to the entity. Definitional drift is the commonest real contradiction, and an unmarked open term is where it starts.
### 04. Unit of account
- Belongs here: the level at which the standard is applied — how a contract, asset or population is divided, combined or grouped before anything else happens to it. Identifying the item; separating a contract into components; combining items into one; grouping or aggregating into the level at which a test is performed; the basis for allocating an amount across those components or groups; the level at which any later test must be applied; criteria for changing the unit afterwards.
- Hand off: whether the standard applies at all -> Scope and applicability. Which category the item falls into once identified -> Classification and categorisation. How the allocated amount is computed -> Measurement. The trigger for a test performed at this level -> Impairment and write-downs.
- Boundary: this section asks WHAT IS THE THING. Classification asks WHICH BUCKET it goes in.
- Weak source if: the unit is left implicit because the standard never names it in a heading. Standards that devote a section to this call it different things every time; the question is the same one.
### 05. Recognition and derecognition
- Belongs here: whether the item is on the books at all. Initial recognition trigger; derecognition criteria in the order they are applied; partial derecognition and continuing involvement; whether a change in terms replaces the item with a new one; recognition as an expense or transfer out on consumption or sale; the derecognition consequence of a write-off.
- Derecognition criteria keep their conditions word for word (for example "when, and only when", or a threshold such as "substantially all"). A test is never recorded without the condition that limits it.
- When the standard sets a test for one kind of item only, record which item it applies to and never extend it to others. If the standard gives no test for a question, record that it is silent rather than borrowing a test from elsewhere.
- Hand off: the amount -> Measurement. The category -> Classification and categorisation. What the item is -> Unit of account. Extinguishing an allowance rather than the item -> Impairment and write-downs. What follows in another section when a change in terms replaces the item with a new one (for example a new start date for a later test) -> the section that owns that consequence.
- Boundary: extinguishing the ITEM is here; extinguishing the ALLOWANCE is impairment. On a modification, whether the item SURVIVES is here; what the new amount is belongs to Measurement.
- Weak source if: the derecognition tests are listed without their applied sequence. The order is part of the requirement.
### 06. Classification and categorisation
- Belongs here: where the standard sorts items into categories that drive later treatment. The classification tests and the order they are applied in; each resulting category and what it commits the entity to; designation options and whether they are irrevocable; reclassification — when permitted, when required, when prohibited, and from what date it takes effect.
- Whether a classification, designation or reclassification is required, permitted or prohibited is kept exactly as the standard states it ("shall", "may", "only if", "irrevocable"). A permitted designation is never recorded as a requirement, and a conditional test is never recorded without its condition.
- A category assigned to an item in an example or illustration is recorded as that example's outcome, naming the example, and never as a general classification rule.
- Hand off: the measurement consequence of a category -> Measurement. A regime with its own qualifying criteria and discontinuation rules -> Elections and special regimes. The policy character of a designation -> also recorded in Judgements.
- Not applicable for many standards, and for some it applies to only one party to the arrangement. Both are correct states; record which.
- Weak source if: a category is recorded without its downstream consequence, or a designation is recorded without whether it can be undone.
### 07. Measurement
- Belongs here: how an amount is computed. Initial measurement including transaction costs; subsequent measurement by category; every measurement basis the standard uses and its arithmetic — INCLUDING a recoverable, realisable or fair amount and the techniques used to reach it; discount rates and present value technique; unwinding, accretion and time-value mechanics, including on an item already written down; the amount following a modification; the amount allocated across units identified in Unit of account; where gains and losses are presented and whether they recycle.
- Where the standard assigns a different rate, basis or input to different kinds of item, each is recorded against its own item and never generalised to all. Permissions and limits on them are kept word for word ("or an approximation", "only if, and to the extent that").
- A presumption and its fallback are recorded together, exactly as stated ("there is a presumption that... in those rare cases... the entity shall use..."). A presumption is recorded as rebuttable only if the standard calls it rebuttable.
- Figures, formulas and calculations taken from an example or illustration are recorded as that example's arithmetic, naming the example, and not as the standard's measurement method. A formula is recorded as the standard's own only if the standard itself states it.
- Hand off: the decision to perform a test and what happens when it fails -> Impairment and write-downs. The basis for splitting across components -> Unit of account. A measurement basis defined by another standard -> that standard, linked and not restated.
- Boundary: an amount representing WHAT THE ITEM IS WORTH is here — recoverable amount, value in use, fair value less costs of disposal, net realisable value. An amount representing THE LOSS is impairment. Interest stays here regardless of impaired status.
- Weak source if: recoverable-amount machinery is filed as impairment merely because its purpose is a loss test.
### 08. Impairment and write-downs
- Belongs here: the decision to test an amount for loss in value, the comparison that test makes, and what follows. What triggers a test and how often; indicators that a test is required; the level at which the test applies; what is compared with what; staging or bucketing; deterioration criteria and rebuttable presumptions; recognition of the loss and its allocation; reversal criteria — permitted, required, or FORBIDDEN; simplified approaches and practical expedients; write-off as the terminal point of the allowance.
- The items to which the test applies are listed here in full, as the standard lists them. A partial list is not recorded as the whole.
- Triggers, thresholds and presumptions keep their exact strength and role as stated — whether the standard calls something a requirement, a presumption, an indicator, an example of an indicator, or the latest point at which a step must occur. A presumption is recorded with what it presumes, what rebuts it, and the evidence needed to rebut it.
- Simplifications, approximations and practical expedients are recorded with every condition under which they are permitted and any circumstance the standard says rules them out.
- An outcome reached in an example or illustration (a party recognising a loss, moving between stages or buckets, or rebutting a presumption) is recorded as that example's outcome, naming the example, and never as a trigger or rule.
- Hand off: computing the amount compared against -> Measurement. Interest on a written-down item -> Measurement. Input estimation methodology -> methodology/research. Client parameters and thresholds -> client working papers. Regulatory floors or add-ons -> supervisory/regulatory. Disclosure of the loss -> Presentation and disclosure.
- Boundary: this section holds the TRIGGER, the COMPARISON, the CONSEQUENCE and the REVERSAL, plus any amount representing THE LOSS ITSELF. Amounts representing what the item is worth belong to Measurement. How the loss amount itself is measured (for example how expected losses are estimated) belongs here, not in Measurement.
- Weak source if: a rebuttable presumption is recorded without what rebuts it, or the reversal position is left unstated. Some standards require reversal, others forbid it; silence is not neutral.
### 09. Elections and special regimes
- Belongs here: optional or conditional regimes sitting alongside the main requirements, each with its own qualifying criteria, mechanics and discontinuation rules. Irrevocable policy choices carrying their own machinery. Temporary reliefs that operate during normal running.
- A regime is kept together: its requirements and its application guidance are filed here as a whole, not split across the other sections it touches. Those sections link to it.
- An election is recorded as optional, exactly as the standard grants it ("may", "may choose", "may elect"), together with the level at which the choice is made (item by item, by class, or for all items) and whether it can be revoked. An election is never recorded as a requirement.
- Qualifying criteria and discontinuation conditions keep their wording in full ("only if", "shall discontinue when", "for as long as"). A regime is never recorded with some of its qualifying criteria but not all of them.
- Hand off: designations that only change a measurement category -> Classification and categorisation. A straight either/or choice with no machinery of its own -> the section that owns the mechanic, with the choice recorded in Judgements. Reliefs available only on first application -> Transition and effective date.
- Boundary: a regime needs all three of qualifying criteria, mechanics, and discontinuation. Missing any one and it is a policy choice, not a regime.
- Weak source if: a regime is captured with how to qualify and how to apply but not when it must stop. Discontinuation is the part most often omitted and the part most often breached.
- Examples that illustrate a regime filed here (for example hedge accounting) are grouped with this section on the Worked examples pages, not with Measurement, even where they show arithmetic.
### 10. Presentation and disclosure
- Belongs here: what appears on the face of the primary statements and what must be told to the reader. Presentation requirements; disclosure requirements whether in this standard or its disclosure partner; where disclosure sits in a separate standard, the link and an explicit statement of how the split runs; disclosure attached to a specific election or regime; disclosure of judgements made.
- Each disclosure keeps its strength as stated: required ("shall disclose"), encouraged or permitted ("is encouraged to", "may"), or required only when a condition is met ("if material", "when the entity has elected"). A disclosure objective is recorded as an objective and is not turned into a list of specific required items, and specific items are not merged into the objective.
- Where the information must appear is recorded exactly as stated — on the face of a primary statement, in the notes, or either — and is not assumed.
- A table, format or wording shown in an example or illustration is recorded as that example's presentation, naming the example, and not as a required format.
- Hand off: the full text of a partner disclosure standard -> that standard's own map. Disclosure required only on first application -> Transition and effective date.
- Weak source if: disclosure is treated as belonging to this standard when it actually lives in a partner standard, or the split between the two is left implicit. Say where disclosure lives in the first line.
### 11. Judgements, estimates and policy choices
- Belongs here: every point at which the standard hands the entity a decision. Each judgement with its reference; each estimate and what it is an estimate of; each policy choice, whether irrevocable, and the level at which it is made; rebuttable presumptions with what rebuts them and the evidential bar; terms the standard leaves to the entity to define; unit-of-account determinations that require judgement; elections recorded alongside their owning section.
- This section is not exclusive. A judgement-creating requirement lives in its subject section AND is recorded here, as Checks (Also recorded) sets out. The duplications that list names are the only sanctioned ones.
- The duplication is by link, not by rewording: each item recorded here is one short line naming the decision point, opening as its paragraph's kind requires (the openers in Writing page text; the Standard's own text has none), with its clause reference and a link to the subject section that holds the full text. The full text is never restated here. This section is written after the other sections.
- A presumption is recorded here only if the standard itself calls it a presumption, and as rebuttable only if the standard says it can be rebutted. A statement is not promoted to a presumption, and a presumption is not promoted to a rebuttable one.
- A judgement is recorded as the decision the standard leaves to the entity, not as the answer an example reached. An example's conclusion is that example's outcome, named as such.
- Hand off: the answer for any given client -> client working papers. The estimation methodology -> methodology/research. The mechanics of an election -> the section that owns it.
- Test: could two entities applying this standard CORRECTLY reach different answers here? If yes, it belongs here whatever else it also belongs to.
- Weak source if: a presumption recorded anywhere else in the map does not also appear here. This is the section a working paper is written against, and a gap here is a gap in the watchdog.
### 12. Transition and effective date
- Belongs here: how an entity gets from the previous requirement to this one, and only that. Effective date and early application conditions; transition approach — full retrospective, modified retrospective, prospective; reliefs and practical expedients available on transition, with expiry; comparative restatement requirements; transition-specific disclosure; transition provisions attached to EACH amendment, recorded separately; withdrawal of superseded pronouncements.
- Each transition claim records the amendment or version it belongs to, so provisions from different amendments can never be merged into one.
- Reliefs and transition requirements keep their strength and limits as stated — required ("shall"), permitted ("may", "is not required to"), conditional ("if ... would require undue cost or effort") — together with when they apply and when they stop.
- A transition rule whose effect continues after first application (for example, applying until an item is derecognised) is recorded with how long it lasts and linked from the section whose requirement it changes.
- Hand off: steady-state requirements -> the functional sections. Reliefs that operate during normal running rather than only at first application -> Elections and special regimes. The bare dates -> also recorded in Overview and status, as Checks (Also recorded) sets out.
- Weak source if: transition provisions from several amendments are merged into one account. They are layered and each amendment carries its own; merging them is the commonest defect in this section.
### 13. Board reasoning
- Belongs here: why the requirements are as they are, keyed to the requirement each passage explains. Alternatives the issuer considered and rejected, with the reason. Concerns raised in consultation and how they were addressed. Dissenting opinions. Effects analysis where published. Reasoning carried forward from a predecessor standard without reconsideration, flagged as such. Comparisons with another framework's equivalent standard where the issuer publishes one.
- NOTHING IN THIS SECTION IS AUTHORITATIVE. It is not a requirement and must never be filed as one.
- Every claim here is worded as the issuer's reasoning, with the issuer as the subject ("The <issuer> considered...", "The <issuer> concluded..."), never as an obligation on the entity.
- A reference to reasoning is not reasoning. Pointers such as "see Basis for Conclusions paragraphs ..." are not claims and are not recorded, here or on the requirement they sit beside. Claims here come only from the reasoning text itself.
- A dissenting opinion is attributed to the member who holds it and is never presented as the issuer's view.
- Hand off: anything readable as a requirement -> the functional section that owns it. Worked fact patterns -> Worked examples and illustrations.
- Boundary: if it argues, it is here. If it works through a fact pattern, it is examples.
- Value: when a working paper appears to conflict with a requirement, this is what settles whether the conflict is real or a misreading. Keep it even though it binds nothing.
- Weak source if: an entry carries the reasoning reference but not the requirement reference it explains. Both are needed or it cannot be found when it matters.
- Not applicable until a source containing the issuer's reasoning (such as a basis for conclusions) has been ingested. Until then the page says it is empty. Text from the standard itself, including its application guidance, is never reworded as the issuer's reasoning.
### 14. Worked examples and illustrations
- Belongs here: the issuer's own examples, each indexed to the requirement it illustrates, with the arithmetic where quantitative. An example is a passage the issuer heads with one of its labels (such as "Example 1" or "Instrument A"); a paragraph that only says "for example", or names a label inside its text, is not.
- This section is split. Its examples are written on subtopic pages, one per section of the fifteen that the examples illustrate: each example goes on the subtopic page of the section that owns the requirement it illustrates. This section's own page is the index of those subtopic pages: under each, one line per example, giving the example's name, what it illustrates (its heading as the issuer prints it) and the clause of the requirement (the Standard's paragraphs its text refers to), with a link to the subtopic page. An example inside application guidance is listed here too, with a link to the page that holds its requirement. The index holds no full write-ups.
- Each example is headed by the issuer's own example number and title (for example "Example 1 – <title>"), not by the paragraph number it starts at. Its paragraph numbers are given in the citations.
- Where an example has no number of its own, it is named by its title instead: "In the illustrative example on <title>, ...". A paragraph number is never used as an example's name.
- An example inside application guidance (its paragraph is application guidance, as in an appendix that is an integral part of the Standard) opens with both: "Application guidance states that, in Example <n>, ...". It binds as application guidance does, and it is filed with the requirement it applies to, not on this section's subtopic pages.
- Every example claim names its example and keeps the parties and facts exactly as given ("In Example 4, Entity A ..."). It is never restated as a general statement about entities.
- The facts and assumptions an example relies on are recorded with its outcome, including any simplifications the example states (for example "for simplicity, it is assumed that ..."). An outcome is never recorded without the facts that produced it.
- Each example claim carries the authority status read from the authority sentence. Example claims are filed only on this section's subtopic pages, except an example inside application guidance; the section that owns the requirement links to the example rather than holding a copy of it.
- AUTHORITY IS NOT ASSUMED. Read it from the standard's own authority sentence, transcribed in Overview and status. Most issuers place examples OUTSIDE the binding scope; some place them inside, in which case they bind. Getting this wrong in either direction is a correctness failure, not a tidiness one.
- Hand off: examples the firm or a third party constructed -> technical guidance, never here. Argument about why a rule exists -> Board reasoning.
- Application guidance in the standard's own appendices is not an example, even where it says "for example". It stays in the section that owns the requirement and is never copied here.
- Not applicable where the issuer publishes no examples with the standard, which is common outside the largest standards. The other sections then correctly carry no example references, and that is complete rather than missing.
- Weak source if: an example is captured without the requirement it illustrates, or its authority is asserted rather than read from the authority sentence.
### 15. Interactions and application in practice
- Belongs here: relationships. Standards this one touches and the nature of each — scope boundary, disclosure partner, measurement input, consequential amendment. Standards this one interprets, where it is an interpretation. Interpretations and agenda decisions issued on this standard, indexed. Comparisons with another framework's equivalent. Pointers out to client working papers, methodology/research, supervisory/regulatory and technical guidance.
- Agenda decisions belong in this store, not in technical guidance. They are issuer output and entities are expected to apply them, but they are not part of the standard. Index them here and record each against the section whose subject it affects.
- An agenda decision or interpretation is worded with its issuing body as the subject ("The <issuing body> concluded that ...", or noted, observed, decided or emphasised), or, where printed as an editorial note, may open "The editorial note states ..."; with its date and the question it answered, and carries statement kind editorial. It is never worded as a requirement of the standard.
- An agenda decision reproduced inside another source (for example, printed as a note in an annotated edition) is an editorial note: it is recorded once, here, cited ED:<clause it sits beside>, naming its issuing body and its date as printed. The section it bears on links to it rather than holding a copy, and repeat printings are not recorded again.
- A requirement of another standard is attributed to that standard by name and is not recorded as a requirement of this one.
- Hand off: whether the standard applies at all -> Scope and applicability. The content of anything linked -> its own store.
- Boundary: scope decides whether; this section describes how, once both apply.
- Weak source if: a link names a standard but not the specific part of it, or an agenda decision is indexed here without being recorded against the section it actually bears on.

## Slugs and links
- Slugs are lowercase kebab-case, unique and stable. The top-level page's slug is overview. A section slug is the framework slug plus the slugified section name (e.g. ifrs-9-impairment-and-write-downs). They come from the fixed vocabulary, so they are never renamed and never invented. Retitling a source changes its title, never its slug.
- Each composed page type has a fixed slug pattern built from its controlled list, never from free text: source-<source-name>, <framework>-entity-<designation>, <framework>-concept-<term or heading> (as Checks (Concept slug) sets out), <framework>-comparison-<subject-a>-vs-<subject-b> (when built), <framework>-synthesis-<question-id> (a short id the engine gives the saved question, the same each time the same question or chat is saved). A subtopic page's slug is <section-slug>-<group>. A subtopic slug uses the heading's own number where it has one: digits, or a letter and digits, joined by full stops (e.g. <section-slug>-6-3, <section-slug>-g-2); otherwise the first five words of the heading, slugified. Two pages that would produce the same slug are the same page.
- Link with [[slug]], and only to a page that exists or is in the fixed vocabulary. Anything else is written as plain text, as set out in Writing page text.
- The overview links every section page. Subtopic pages are reached from their section's index page or from the page list.
- Composed pages may be read when answering, like any page on the page list, but are never used to choose where a claim is filed. Saved answers are not on the page list; they are read alongside, as Answer and check sets out.
- Composed pages refer to claims by clause reference (and claim ID where the engine stores claims), not by copying the claim's text. Rendering a reference to a discarded or superseded claim as a dead reference, so a stale citation is visible, is not built yet (a future feature; needs the claims store).
- Display text is written [[slug|display text]]. The part before the pipe is the page slug; the part after is what the reader sees. Citations use this form to show the clause: [[source-<source-name>|<clause reference>]].

## Page content
- A section body is rebuilt from the rendered claims filed to it and never hand-edited. The rebuild clears as well as writes, so a section whose claims have gone says so rather than keeping what it last said.
- Every statement shows its clause reference, and every statement from application guidance, implementation guidance, an example, an editorial note or the basis for conclusions says what it is in its wording, as set out in Writing page text, so its authority is visible wherever it appears.
- pages.summary is derived — the first three rendered claims, on one line; a subtopic page's summary is drawn across its headings, as Checks sets out. pages.description, generated from the page's claims (what the page covers, as opposed to a fact from it) and written in the same statement as description_grounding — the top-15 term counts and the statement-kind split it was built from — so the two cannot disagree, is not built yet (a future feature). Where a page holds requirement claims, the summary is drawn from those first, so a page is never summarised by an example or an annotation.
- Claims are tagged by statement kind, read from the clause the quote sits in rather than from the file it came from: requirement, application guidance, implementation guidance, example or editorial. A question about what the standard requires is answered only from requirement claims, so it can never be answered with an illustration or an annotation.
- Not built yet (a future feature; needs the claims store): Verification compares each claim with its quote. It checks that the claim is supported, and that the claim keeps the quote's force (required, permitted, expected, typically) and its named parties. A claim that fails is rewritten once from its quote; if it fails again, the quote itself becomes the claim. Unsupported claims are kept for audit and never rendered; a claim is rendered only once it is fully supported.
- Composed pages are rebuilt the same way: from the claims they cite, cleared as well as written, and flagged when a cited claim changes or is withdrawn.

## Contradictions and updates
- A contradiction is recorded as a row with both positions and the authority that favours one, and both sides are surfaced at query time rather than one being silently preferred. Contradiction rows and their callouts are not built yet (a future feature); until they are, a conflict is stated in the answer as Answer and check sets out.
- Before a contradiction is recorded, the two claims are compared through their quotes. If the quotes agree and only the claims' wording differs, it is a rewriting error: the claims are regenerated from their quotes and no contradiction is recorded.
- Where two claims of different statement kind conflict, the authority that favours one follows the kind: a requirement outranks application guidance, which outranks implementation guidance, an example or an editorial annotation. Both positions are still surfaced, with the ranking stated.
- A recorded contradiction is shown on every page holding either claim, as: > [!warning] Contradiction: <source A> says X; <source B> says Y. — with the ranking by statement kind where one applies.
- Not built yet (a future feature): Supersession is explicit: replaced_by_source_id and effective_to date the old claim rather than deleting it. Each claim records the amendment or version it comes from, so a claim from a later amendment supersedes the earlier one explicitly rather than appearing as a contradiction.
- Not built yet (a future feature): When a claim is superseded, the page shows the current claim with a note beneath it: > [!note] Updated: <new source/amendment>, effective <date>, replaces <old source>. Changed: <what changed>. Reason: <the amendment or source that changed it>. The superseded claim is kept, dated, and not edited in place.
- Cross-store findings are dispositioned through a review step with a named actor, never by editing a claim in place.
- Nothing is deleted to resolve a disagreement. The old assertion stays, dated, so the record shows what changed and when.

## Style
- Plain, precise language. No filler. Short paragraphs and lists.
- A claim is one verifiable statement with a verbatim quote. Never invent or infer beyond the text.
- A claim keeps the subject and the force of its quote. A named party stays named and is never turned into "an entity" or "you"; shall/must, may/can, should and generally/typically are carried over unchanged; conditions such as "only if", "unless" and "except" are kept; a presumption is not made rebuttable unless the quote says so.
- Claims from application guidance, implementation guidance, examples, editorial notes, the introduction or the basis for conclusions say what they are in their wording: "Application guidance states ...", "The implementation guidance states ...", "In Example <n>, <party> ...", "The editorial note states ...", "The introduction states ...", "The dissenting opinion of <member> states ...", "The <issuer> concluded ...", "The <issuing body> concluded ..."; from an agenda decision printed as an editorial note, "The <issuing body> concluded ...", "The <issuing body> noted ...", "The <issuing body> observed ...", "The <issuing body> decided ...", "The <issuing body> emphasised ..." or "The editorial note states ...". Claims from the Standard's own paragraphs carry no such wording, whatever their force.
- A claim states content. A heading, a table fragment, or a sentence that only points elsewhere ("Paragraph X governs Y", "Section Z contains the requirements") is not a claim.
- Be exhaustive within a section — if it supports ten claims, return ten — but a section that genuinely states little should return little. Exhaustive means every point once: the same point from the same clause is not recorded twice.
- An honest empty answer must stay expressible. Zero pages, and zero claims on a page, are answers; the schema never forces a claim where the source states none.

## Writing page text
These rules apply every time the maintainer writes or rewrites page text, whether or not the engine stores separate claims.
- Cite the clause on every sentence that states something from a source. End each such sentence with [[source-<source-name>|<clause reference>]]. A citation to the whole document with no clause reference is not allowed. A sentence drawn from two clauses cites both.
- Sentences about the wiki itself (what a section covers, whether it holds requirements, what the overview lists) describe the wiki, not a source, and carry no source citation. A page number or the start of a source is never cited for them. A line that applies to every section is written once, not repeated under each.
- Use the source's own force words. shall, must, may, can, should, might, for example, typically and generally are copied exactly as the source uses them. "May be considered" never becomes "is", and "might, for example" never becomes "often".
- Write up what each source paragraph actually says: its definitions, numbers, thresholds, time periods, conditions, exceptions and who must do what. A mention or a citation alone is not a write-up.
- Keep every condition, word for word. "Only if", "unless", "except", "subject to <clause>", "regardless of", thresholds, time limits and similar limits stay in the sentence they limit. A condition is never dropped, shortened or moved away from what it limits, and a list of conditions keeps every item with the source's own lettering ((a), (b), (i), (ii)).
- Say what kind of paragraph a sentence comes from. A sentence from application guidance opens "Application guidance states ..."; from implementation guidance, "The implementation guidance states ..."; from an example, "In Example <n>, ..."; from an editorial note, "The editorial note states ..."; from the introduction, "The introduction states ..."; from a dissenting opinion, "The dissenting opinion of <member> states ..."; from the basis for conclusions, "The <issuer> concluded ..."; from an agenda decision printed as an editorial note, "The <issuing body> concluded ...", "The <issuing body> noted ...", "The <issuing body> observed ...", "The <issuing body> decided ...", "The <issuing body> emphasised ..." or "The editorial note states ...". A sentence from the Standard's own paragraphs has no opener, whatever its force: a requirement ("shall"), a permission ("may"), an objective or a presumption is written without one. The opener is chosen by where the paragraph comes from, never by its force. A short linking word may come before the opener (for example "However, application guidance states ..."). An example inside application guidance opens with both: "Application guidance states that, in Example <n>, ...". A list item that continues a lead-in sentence takes its opener from that lead-in. A sentence drawn from clauses of different kinds opens each part with what that part is.
- Quote where the wording matters. Where a short phrase carries the meaning (a defined term, a threshold, a presumption), put it in quotation marks exactly as the source writes it.
- Say each point once. A point is written once, on the page whose section brief covers it, and only once on that page: a clause already written up is not restated in a summary, a list or a later sub-heading. A sentence drawn from several clauses cites them all, each by its own label, instead of being repeated for each; a range ("<a>–<b>") is a pointer, not a write-up of the paragraphs inside it. Other pages link to that page rather than restating the point.
- Stay inside the section. A page holds only what its section brief covers. Content that belongs to another section is written there, not here.
- Link only to pages that exist. Other standards and documents are written as plain text, by designation only and always in the same form, unless a page for them exists.
- Never state facts about the wiki from memory. Dates come from the log or the source record; counts are counted from the pages, not estimated. If a figure is not known, it is left out.
- One overview page, slug overview. No second top-level page is created under the framework slug or any other name.
- Each section is broken into sub-headings by subject (for example “Loss allowance”, “Significant increase in credit risk”, “Measurement”), so no block of text runs for more than a few hundred words. Sub-headings follow the standard’s own headings where it has them. Sub-headings are by subject, never one heading per paragraph number.

## Workflow: Ingest
- Register the source before anything is extracted: issuer, designation, edition, part, and a content hash. The source page is created only if the designation resolves. A source whose hash matches one already ingested is not ingested again. A new hash for the same designation being a new version, whose claims supersede the old ones under Contradictions and updates rather than being added beside them, is not built yet (a future feature).
- Read the authority sentence first and record it in Overview and status. Then assign every part of the source a tier: binding text, application guidance, implementation guidance, examples and illustrations, reasoning, or editorial material. The tier of the part a quote comes from sets the claim's statement kind. A part of no kind listed in Checks (Paragraph kinds or Kinds left out) is binding text, as the authority sentence makes it (e.g. an appendix the sentence names).
- Separate editorial material from the standard's text before any claim is drawn: bracketed notes, cross-reference boxes, footnoted annotations and reproduced agenda decisions. They are kept and quoted only as editorial claims, never as part of the clause they sit beside, and never dropped unless Checks (Kinds left out) names their kind. A bare pointer ("[Refer: ...]") is not a claim.
- Split the text into clauses using the source's own labels, and give every claim one clause reference in a fixed form: numbered paragraphs exactly as the source prints them, including sub-paragraph letters; application guidance, examples and reasoning by their own printed labels; defined terms as "Appendix A: <term>" (or the glossary's own name where it has one); editorial notes as ED:<clause they sit beside>, including an agenda decision reproduced inside the source; agenda decisions and interpretations published as documents of their own as AD:<issuer>-<date>. A quote that crosses two clauses becomes two claims. Text the source prints without a paragraph label is cited as ED:<clause it sits beside> if it is an editorial note; front matter with no paragraph label (such as the authority sentence), and the source's own text printed without a paragraph label (such as a closing line under its defined terms), are the only text cited by its page label.
- Tables: a table that states requirements, definitions or required disclosures is claimed row by row, each claim carrying the table's reference and its column headings. A table of figures inside an example stays with that example. Layout fragments, headings and running heads are discarded.
- Extract claims against the section briefs. Every claim carries its quote, character offsets, clause reference, statement kind, subject, force (required, permitted, expected, typically, illustration) and the amendment or version it comes from.
- Verify every claim as set out in Page content, then file it to the section its brief routes it to. A claim that names no section of the fifteen is discarded and counted.
- Route application guidance with the requirement it applies. A B paragraph goes to the section that holds the chapter its number points to (B5.5.x with chapter 5.5, B6.x with chapter 6), unless a section brief hands it off. Application guidance that says "for example" is still application guidance, not an example: it goes to the section that owns its requirement, not to the examples section. Implementation guidance is routed the same way: to the section that owns the requirement it explains, never to the examples section. Within a split section, application guidance goes to the sub-page of the requirement it applies to, not to a sub-page of its own appendix heading.
- Where a section is split, a paragraph routed to it is filed to the subtopic page of its group, as that section's brief sets out. A paragraph from a document published with the Standard (implementation guidance, illustrative examples) goes to the subtopic page of the requirement it refers to, by the paragraph reference in its heading or text; a labelled example still goes to the examples section, and a footnote goes with the paragraph it belongs to. A paragraph with no such reference goes under its nearest real heading, never under the document's title heading. The section's own page is then written as the index of its subtopic pages.
- Check coverage: every clause of binding text has at least one claim, or is recorded as stating nothing that can be claimed. A clause is covered only where a sentence cites it as its own source, in the group of citations that ends the sentence (a sentence drawn from several clauses covers each of them); a link to a paragraph in passing inside a sentence is a cross-reference, a pointer and not a claim, and covers nothing; a range names only its two ends (Checks: Range citations). Gaps are listed in the ingest report, never written on a page and never filled by inference.
- Rebuild the affected pages, then run the checks in Workflow: Maintain. An ingest that fails those checks is not exported: it is held for review, with each failing page flagged, and is applied only by a named reviewer or after the cause is fixed and the source uploaded again.

## Workflow: Answer and check
- To answer, choose pages from the page list, read them, and follow links (a section's index to its subtopic pages, and cross-links) until the question is answered. Cite only what supports the answer; list only cited pages. Where a question covers more than one section, answer each part from the page that covers it. A question about what the standard requires is answered from requirement claims; any other kind used in the answer is labelled as what it is. Under the Sources setting "Standard only" (Checks: Standard only), every page is read with only its statements from the Standard's own text, chosen by paragraph kind, not by source document.
- Every statement in an answer rests on a claim and cites its clause reference (and claim ID where the engine stores claims). Citing a whole document or page is not a citation. Nothing is stated that no claim supports.
- A paragraph is cited in an answer as on a page: only by the group of citations that ends the sentence it supports. A link inside a sentence, to a concept, entity or other page, or to a paragraph in passing, is a pointer, not a citation. A link to a page stays as the link or its plain term and is never replaced by a paragraph citation. The clause references list only the citations that end the answer's sentences.
- An answer keeps each claim's force, subject and conditions. A permitted step is not presented as required, an example's outcome is not presented as a rule, and a condition is not dropped to shorten the answer.
- Before saying the standard does not address something, search every section, including Definitions, for the terms involved. Only then say it is not addressed, and say what was searched for. The engine searches the text of every section page and subtopic page, Definitions included, for each point the answer says is not addressed, before the answer is final; a point the search finds material for is answered from that material, and each point left as not addressed names the terms searched for and the sections searched.
- Answers are laid out as: the answer; the clause references relied on; anything the standard does not address; and, kept separate, any non-authoritative material (implementation guidance, examples, reasoning, editorial notes, agenda decisions, the introduction) with its kind stated. Application guidance is authoritative: it stays in the answer, with its opener. The headings are those in Checks (Answer layout); a part with nothing in it says so under its heading.
- Every answer is checked before it is given: each sentence that states something from a source cites a clause; a sentence citing a paragraph of a kind listed in Paragraph kinds carries that kind's opener (Checks: Openers); a force word is the one its cited paragraph uses (Checks: Answer force words), and no obligation is stated that the cited paragraph does not state; the layout is as Checks (Answer layout) sets out. An answer that fails is sent back once with the list of failures. Failures still left are shown with the answer.
- Where the pages read give conflicting positions, the answer states both, each with its clause, and ranks them by kind as Checks (Kind ranking) sets out, as Contradictions and updates requires.
- Checking a client statement (the watchdog): find the clause or clauses the statement relies on, compare the statement with their claims and quotes, and give one verdict from Checks (Client-statement verdicts). Every verdict cites the clause and quotes the passage it rests on, word for word from the source.
- A client document never changes a claim in this store. Findings that bear on the store go through the review step in Contradictions and updates: a finding is recorded with the statement, its verdict, the clause and the quote, and is closed only by a named reviewer, whose name and decision are recorded with it.
- Saved answers are read alongside the pages, never instead of them, and are never cited. A saved answer flagged as needing checking (its cited pages have changed) or saved with citation problems is not read when answering, and not used by any other step, until it is checked or saved again.
- An answer worth keeping is filed as a synthesis page under the rules in Page types.

## Workflow: Maintain
- After every ingest, and before export, run these checks. All must pass:
  1. every claim has a clause reference and a quote that is found at its offsets in the source (not built yet: needs the claims store);
  2. no claim from an editorial, implementation guidance, example or reasoning part carries statement kind requirement;
  3. no claim is a heading, a table fragment or a pointer only;
  4. no two claims share a clause and overlapping offsets (not built yet: needs the claims store);
  5. no unsupported claim is rendered;
  6. every claim that failed the force or named-party check was rewritten or replaced by its quote (not built yet: needs the claims store);
  7. every binding clause has a claim or is recorded as stating nothing claimable.
- An ingest that fails is not exported: it is held for review, not applied. The failures are reported by check and by section, and each failing page is flagged. A held ingest is applied only by a named reviewer, or the cause is fixed and the checks run again.
- After a page is written, it is checked against the lists in Checks: every lettered or numbered part and every condition phrase of each source paragraph appears; every sentence from application guidance, implementation guidance, an example, an editorial note, the introduction or the basis for conclusions carries its opener; a source page has its listed parts and no more than the allowed key takeaways; every paragraph marked for the Judgements section has its line; and no citation is flagged. Subtopic pages are checked one by one, like any topic page. A page that fails is sent back, at most twice, paragraph by paragraph: each paragraph of the page that a failure points to is rewritten on its own, with its failures. A paragraph's rewrite is kept when it clears the failures that point only to it, does not worsen the failures it shares with other paragraphs, adds no new failure and loses no citation. A failure that points to no paragraph is sent back with the whole page, and that rewrite is kept when it has fewer failures and loses no citation. After each round the whole page is checked again. A paragraph whose write-up still fails after the second round is written as its own words, quoted in full, with its opener and citation: the quote itself becomes the claim. Concept pages are not checked as section pages are: their lines are checked only for citations that name a paragraph the source holds and links to pages that exist.
- Each ingest reports, outside this schema: claims by statement kind and by section, verification results, claims replaced by their quote, claims discarded by reason, and coverage gaps.
- Synthesis and comparison pages whose cited claims have changed are rebuilt or flagged stale. Contradictions and supersessions are handled as set out in Contradictions and updates.
- Lint reports; it never edits a page. Each of the checks above that lint runs is named as Checks (Lint checks) sets out, and each it cannot run is reported as not run, with the reason, never passed. A link lint would add or remove is reported as a proposal, with the page and the link; pages change only through an ingest or a rebuild. Lint reads only topic, subtopic, source, overview, concept and entity pages: saved answers are never its material. A page that holds only the Empty section text is not a thin page.
- A page list given to the model (each page's slug, type, title and summary) holds every page. Where it is too long for one call, it is given in parts, every page in one of them; it is never cut.

## Checks
The engine reads these lists to route the issuer's labelled examples, to split sections into subtopic pages and, after a page is written, to check it as set out in Workflow: Maintain. It reads the words only from here. Every list below is present; a list that is not used says "none". A list that is missing, or whose wording the engine cannot read, is an error in the ingest report and holds the upload for review.
- Paragraph kinds (as the PDF reader names them): appendix_b = application guidance; implementation_guidance = implementation guidance; example = example; basis_for_conclusions = basis for conclusions; note = editorial note; footnote = editorial note; intro = introduction; dissenting_opinion = dissenting opinion
- Kinds left out (as the PDF reader names them; never stored or written up): back_matter
- Openers: application guidance = "Application guidance states"; implementation guidance = "The implementation guidance states"; example = "In <example label>,", "In the illustrative example on <title>,"; editorial note = "The editorial note states"; agenda decision = "The <issuing body> concluded", "The <issuing body> noted", "The <issuing body> observed", "The <issuing body> decided", "The <issuing body> emphasised", "The editorial note states"; introduction = "The introduction states"; dissenting opinion = "The dissenting opinion of <member> states"; basis for conclusions = "The <issuer> concluded", "The <issuer> considered"
- Linking words allowed before an opener: "However,", "In addition,", "For example,", "Alternatively,", "Similarly,", "Furthermore,", "Nevertheless,", "Conversely,", "Hence,", "Consequently,", "Accordingly,", "Instead,", "Moreover,", "In contrast,", "In that case,", "Specifically,". Any lead-in of up to four words ending in a comma may come before an opener; the list above gives examples.
- Lettered part forms: "(a)", "(i)"
- Condition phrases: "only if", "unless", "except", "subject to", "regardless of", "despite", "notwithstanding"
- Authority forms: "is set out in paragraphs ... and ...", "This appendix is an integral part of the Standard", "accompanies, but is not part of"
- Source-page parts: what the document is = issuer, edition, part; authority sentence = authority forms; sections it informs = informed sections; key takeaways as linked claims = cited claims
- Key takeaways: at most 5
- Examples section: 14
- Kinds routed to the examples section: example
- Example labels (an issuer's own example, routed to the examples section; <n> is a number or a letter, as in Instrument A): "Example <n>", "Instrument <n>", "Scenario <n>", "Illustration <n>"
- Split sections (section number = what each subtopic page is grouped by): 14 = the section of the fifteen that owns the requirement each example illustrates; 11 = the section each line points to. An item matching Example labels is never split across pages; a group over the size limit splits between items only.
- Document names (a name, never a clause, where it stands in a citation): "IFRS <n>", "IAS <n>", "IFRIC <n>", "SIC <n>"
- Defined-term citation form: "Appendix A: <term>"
- Empty section text: "This section is empty: no paragraph of the sources uploaded so far belongs to it."; its summary "Empty: no source content yet"
- Citation forms for text without a paragraph label: editorial note = "ED:<clause>" (before the first clause, "ED:<page label>"); introduction = "<page label>"; front matter = "<page label>"; the source's own text = "<page label>"
- Range citations: a citation of a range ("<a>–<b>") cites only the two paragraphs it names
- Page size limit: 6000 words. A section page whose write-up would be over this is split by source headings: the highest heading level at which its paragraphs fall under more than one heading. A subtopic page over the limit is split again at the next heading level. After writing, a sub-page is split again only if it is more than 10% over the limit. A document's title heading is not a heading group for splitting. When a section page that already holds write-ups is split, each write-up moves word for word to the sub-page whose heading is on the heading path of the paragraph it cites; where no sub-page has such a heading, it goes to a sub-page named after that paragraph's nearest heading (e.g. 6.6 Presentation), never after the document's title.
- Subtopic page minimum: 1500 words. A group under this is joined to the most related group under the same parent heading: first a group it refers to or that refers to it, then the group closest to it in meaning; the group before it on a tie. A join that would take a group over the page size limit goes to the next most related group instead; if every join would go over, the small group stays as its own page.
- Subtopic slug: the heading's own number where it has one (e.g. 6.3, G.2), otherwise its first 5 words; a page that holds several headings takes the heading that holds most of its words
- Subtopic join: most related
- Joined subtopic slug: largest heading
- Guidance filed with its requirement: application guidance, implementation guidance. Within a split section, a paragraph of these kinds goes to the subtopic page of the requirement it applies to (a paragraph of no kind listed in Paragraph kinds), chosen by reading both; a paragraph that applies to no requirement there keeps its own heading.
- Notes filed with their clause: editorial note. A passage of this kind goes to the section, and the subtopic page, of the clause in its citation (ED:<clause>), unless it is an agenda decision. One cited by its page label, or beside a clause that no section holds, is routed on its own.
- Agenda decision marker (an editorial note whose text contains it is an agenda decision; it is routed as Interactions and application in practice sets out, and keeps the kind editorial note): "Agenda Decision"
- Subtopic summary: "Covers: " followed by the source headings its paragraphs sit under that its title does not name, in document order, each without a bracketed paragraph reference, separated by "; "; then the first requirement claim under each of its 3 largest headings (by words), in page order, on one line; a page with one heading takes its first 3 claims, requirement claims first. At most 500 characters, headings first. A page whose own text is only lines that each point to another page holds no claims of its own: it takes the page summary.
- Page summary: a page that no other summary rule covers, or whose rule gives nothing, has as its summary "Covers: " followed by what the page holds, in page order, separated by "; ": on the page whose claims define the wiki's terms (the page that cites most of the terms cited in the Defined-term citation form), each term it defines; on a source page, the sections it informs (the section pages it links, by their names); on a page whose own text is only lines that each point to another page, each line's own words before its link; on any other page, its own headings, leaving out the heading of lines recorded from other pages. Then its first requirement claims, whole, while they fit. At most 1,000 characters, whole items only, items first. An empty page keeps its empty-page line, and a section index page keeps its index line.
- Stub forms: [Deleted] · <...> not included in this edition · [These paragraphs refer to amendments ... not included]. A paragraph or lettered part matching a stub form is not written up and not checked. A stub inside the text is read as its own paragraph.
- Editorial brackets (the source's own bracketed asides, read by the PDF reader; never part of a paragraph's own text): note = "[Note: ...]"; pointer = "[Refer...]", "[Link...]", "[now...]", "[Note: ... refer to ...]" (a pointer only when the note is one sentence). A note is kept as an editorial note beside its clause. A pointer only points to other text, is never content, and is removed wherever it appears.
- No-content length: 5 words. An editorial note, or text cited by its page label, with this many words or fewer once its pointers are removed, or that is only the source's own headings in a row (a contents list), holds no content: it is not stored, written up or quoted.
- Page-turn text (printed where a box runs over a page; removed wherever it appears): "continued......continued", "continued......"
- Note markers (each starts a note of its own, beside the same clause; <n> is a number): "E<n> ["
- Also recorded (section number = what is also recorded there, besides its main page; one short line each, naming the point, opening as its paragraph's kind requires, with its clause reference and a link to the page that holds the full text): 11 = a paragraph that creates a judgement, an estimate, a policy choice, a presumption or an election, or leaves a term to the entity; 01 = of the paragraphs routed to 12, a paragraph that states the date from which an entity shall apply a requirement (an effective date), not how it is applied
- Concept pages: each defined term cited in the Defined-term citation form on the page that defines the wiki's terms; and each major heading of a source's own text (paragraphs of no kind listed in Paragraph kinds): the highest heading level whose headings carry a number of more than one part (e.g. 5.5), or a source's top heading level where none does, taken only where its lines sit on at least 3 pages. The heading that holds the defined terms is not a concept; each of its terms is.
- Concept lines: one line per clause per holding page, written <clause citation> (<the source heading the clause sits under, as the source prints it>) — [[<holding page>|<its title>]], under the name of the section that holds the page, sections in their order, lines in page order. A term's lines are the claims whose sentence uses the term; a heading's lines are the claims that cite a paragraph under that heading. A term's page opens with one line linking its definition: Defined in [[<the page that defines the wiki's terms>]] — <its citation>.
- Concept term match: whole words, ignoring case, longest term first, with or without a final "s"
- Concept slug: <framework>-concept-<term>; for a heading, the heading's own number where it has one (e.g. <framework>-concept-5-5), otherwise its first 5 words
- Concept summary: "Covers: " followed by the sections its lines sit in, by their names, in section order, separated by "; ". At most 500 characters, whole items only.
- Entity pages: each designation in one of the Document names forms that the pages name, other than one of the wiki's own sources, taken only where its lines sit on at least 2 pages
- Entity lines: as Concept lines; an entity's lines are the claims whose sentence names it in one of its Document names forms
- Entity summary: as Concept summary
- Pages that hold no claims: overview, section index pages, concept pages, entity pages. The overview links every section page; a section index page links every subtopic page of its section. A sentence on such a page that cites a source paragraph is a claim: it moves, word for word, to the page that holds that paragraph (the subtopic page where the section is split); where that page already cites the paragraph, the sentence is dropped. A concept or entity page's lines are references, not claims: they are never moved or dropped.
- Skipped headings: none
- Document control headers: none
